Lifecycle Completion

Our most relatable novel, by far.

Claire Nakamura works in HR at a health insurance company where every executive on the org chart is a psychopath — and she has the diagnostic tools on her desk to prove it. When a member dies because a doctor spent ninety seconds on his file, she stops observing and starts engineering. The problem with removing one psychopath is that the next one gets promoted. So she finds a way to make them destroy each other, all at once. Three executives. One circular firing squad. A cheerful actuarial analyst sends a metrics email right in the middle of it.

The Case of the Borrowed Alibi

This novel brings the classic Perry Mason formula — bought alibis, courtroom traps, Della and Paul running the case with Perry. The characters are drawn from the Raymond Burr television series of the late 1950s and 1960s rather than the Gardner paperbacks. While it honors the courtroom traps and high-stakes investigations of Erle Stanley Gardner, it adds a modern twist: the reader follows the investigation with the “Columbo” license of seeing the pieces fall into place. The thrill isn’t just in the “Who,” but in the “How” — watching Mason, Street, and Drake dismantle a perfect crime before a jury’s eyes.

Summer 1971. A Hollywood stuntman walks into Perry Mason’s office with a confession that could end his career — and send him to prison. He’s been selling alibis for a powerful studio producer’s Tuesday nights, and now that producer’s wife has been found strangled in their Hollywood Hills home. Hamilton Burger has an airtight case: a $500,000 insurance motive, a private security code known to only a few, and a paid-for alibi that the producer can’t admit is a lie without admitting to murder. But Mason suspects the truth is buried deeper than a simple domestic betrayal. To save his client and find the real killer, Perry, Della Street, and Paul Drake must navigate the backlots of Argonaut Pictures, untangling a web of studio embezzlement and long-held secrets.

The Observation Car

This is not a locked-room puzzle built for Poirot. It is a cozy mystery in the tradition of Richard Osman’s Thursday Murder Club — warm, domestic, and deceptively sharp. And similar to Osman’s “Joyce” chapters, our odd-numbered chapters are narrated by Hazel, a retired midwife and school secretary in her early seventies who notices everything. Her voice runs on tea, biscuits, and quiet observation. The even-numbered chapters show what she can’t see.

Three retired Americans — a school-secretary/midwife, a U.S. Marshal, and an actuary — have been traveling together since retirement: cruises, national parks, anywhere with legroom and a decent breakfast. This time it’s a luxury train through the Rockies, a trip Hazel’s late husband planned and never got around to. The charter belongs to a billionaire corporate raider who dies in his locked suite before the Welcome Dinner. The official verdict is altitude sickness. They know better. What begins as a parlor puzzle on rails becomes personal when the murder connects to a thirty-year-old bridge collapse that touched all their lives — and every suspect on the train has their own reason to want the man dead. Then a snowstorm strands them in the mountains, and the only people who can solve it are a woman who notices things while offering shortbread with lemon zest, a man who hasn’t lost his instincts, and a man who trusts numbers more than people. The killer is someone they like. The truth is something none of them wanted to find.

Standing Ground

A government analyst in Washington discovers that a mining conglomerate is destroying communities to seize their land — and that his own agency is helping bury the evidence. He blows the whistle. The agency and the corporation close ranks. His career doesn’t survive. He flies to Central Australia to disappear — to a cattle station in the red desert owned by his cousin, where a quiet stockman named Ted keeps the place going and pastoral families connected, and a native named Miro keeps the bores running. But the same company is here too, pressuring fourteen pastoral families off their leases through shell companies, sabotaged infrastructure, and men who arrive in trucks with no dust on them. Arthur isn’t the type to watch it happen twice. This time he has something he didn’t have in Washington: people who will stand their ground.

The Uncatalogued Book

This book is perhaps our most difficult to read because every paragraph serves two goals at once:
  1. sound as if were spoken by a tradesman in rare manuscripts living in the 1600's;
  2. keep the meaning easily understandable by someone living in the 2000's.
For this reason we also provide a COMPANION_GUIDE.pdf to accompany the book. In a small nod to Gore Vidal’s Creation, Niccolò crosses paths with a few famous figures of his century along the way.

Our narrator is Niccolò, a book trader in his eighties, who is passing what he knows to his young apprentice, Tommaso. As a young man in the trade he learned its hard secret: the most valuable books are the banned ones. Power fears the truth, and the truth is most often written by those it has labeled heretics. For sixty years he is sent wherever books break loose — failing monasteries, plague estates, collapsing courts — because the great buying happens where power is coming apart, and a throne’s fear is the one thing that reliably doubles a book’s price.
His journey carries him east across the whole century: Venice under interdict, Galileo selling his spyglass to the Senate, Constantinople, the Persian roads, Isfahan, and at last Mughal Delhi, where a doomed prince has spent his fortune translating the Hindus’ hidden books — and where, in 1659, Niccolò makes the purchase of his life out of the wreckage of an execution, and can never enter it in any catalogue.
That purchase is why he is talking at all. Tommaso has asked about one Persian manuscript that appears on no shelf list, and the answer is the whole tale.

Juror Nine

A contract killer draws jury duty he can't dodge — and ends up seated as Juror Nine at the murder trial of the very man he was hired to kill. A short, quietly unsettling crime novel about a professional, day by day, watching his target become a person.

Dan Mercer, a history professor at a Nashville university, is also a hitman. Nashville is his quiet base — he travels elsewhere for contracts, keeps his cover clean, eats at the same Thai place four blocks from his apartment. Then a local job comes in: a planning commissioner named Travis Boone who’s blocking a Music Row development. Before Dan can execute the contract, Boone is arrested for murder — framed with his own stolen gun by the same people who hired Dan. And then Dan gets a jury summons he can’t skip without cracking the cover he’s spent eleven years building. He’s seated as Juror Nine. His target is at the defendant’s table. Dan needs Travis acquitted so he can fulfill the contract — but the trial forces him to see the man clearly, through testimony about a historic recording studio Travis tried to protect and witnesses who lived the civil rights history Travis uncovered. Dan knows what a first kill looks like, and this isn’t one. His target shifts, but his methods don’t change.

Makena’s Walk

A friend gave me a copy of “Elephant Company” about Billy Williams who worked in colonial Burma as a “forest man” for a British teak company in 1920. He was amazed by the intelligence and character exhibited by elephants who performed tasks like hauling logs through the jungle. He became the “elephant wallah”. It was fascinating to learn about many of the traits and I have tried to incorporate some of that information into this book.

1965 Kenya. A ceremonial elephant named Makena has walked away from her handlers — not stolen, not lost, but retired. She picked a direction and left with the certainty of someone who’d been planning it. Nairobi’s first major cultural ceremony since independence needs her to lead the procession, and the clock is running. Two better-equipped expeditions have come back empty. A smaller one is hired to find her and bring her back across the difficult country. They find her. She’s calm, healthy, and content. She does not want to return. The group fractures over what they owe the animal versus what they owe the community counting on them. A serious mission with serious people and clever animals, all fun to watch.

Against the Sun

A Cherokee survivor of the Trail of Tears walks out of the wilderness and into a Comanche camp on the Texas plains. He carries a proposal: travel east to Washington and the Hermitage to kill the men responsible for the forced removal of his people. Four Comanche — who have their own debts to settle with the army — agree to go with him. It is 1838. Andrew Jackson has retired to his plantation outside Nashville. Martin Van Buren is president. Joel Poinsett is secretary of war. Together they enforced Jackson’s Indian Removal, ruled illegal by the Supreme Court but carried out anyway. Five Indians ride east to make them answer for it.

The Dead Letter Men

A rider vanishes on the Pony Express trail in central Nevada. His horse is gone, his mail pouch is tucked under sage at the road’s edge, and there is no blood. The company sends two men to find out what happened — a tracker who reads dirt and a lawyer who reads people. What they find is a body in the mountains and a forgery operation running through a relay station: sealed letters opened, financial documents swapped for fakes, and sent on as though nothing changed. The mail isn’t being stolen. It’s being turned into a weapon. They learn who is doing it before long. Then they figure out how. But the why won’t come, and when it does, it is worse than anything they imagined. It is spring 1861. Fort Sumter has fallen. The country is cracking apart. And two men a thousand miles from anywhere that matters are standing in the middle of it.

Scopes 2.0

This novella was not written as a serious literary undertaking. It was a small experiment in AI-assisted fiction — a test bed for trying different prompting techniques, voice calibrations, and collaborative workflows between human and machine. The writing styles may shift as a result. Think of it as a lab notebook that happens to tell a story.

The story it tells: In 2024, Tennessee passes a law banning teaching evolution in public schools. Sarah Chen, a high school biology teacher in the small town of Oakville, teaches it anyway and is arrested in her classroom. What follows is a courtroom drama that mirrors the 1925 Scopes Monkey Trial — same state, same statute, same collision between science and statute — played out a century later under fluorescent lights, with a Fitbit tracking the defendant’s heart rate.
